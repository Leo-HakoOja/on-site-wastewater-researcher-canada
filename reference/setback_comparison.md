# Cross-Jurisdictional Comparison — Horizontal Setbacks

_Last updated: 2026-05-29_

A focused comparison of **minimum horizontal setback distances** required
between a soil-based dispersal area (treatment field / leaching bed) and two
universally-regulated features:

1. a **drinking-water well**, and
2. **surface water** (lake, stream, watercourse).

These are the two most directly comparable hard-numbered rules across Canadian
on-site regimes — a good entry point for a comparative analysis of how the
provinces and territories differ.

> **How to read this file.** Values below are minimums quoted from each
> jurisdiction's primary source (regulation or standard practice manual).
> Many regulations specify **multiple** values depending on the feature type
> (private vs. community well; classified vs. unclassified surface water;
> sealed vs. unsealed groundwater facility; dispersal type; soil class). The
> matrix shows the **baseline** a typical residential design would face.
> Always read the source for the specific case before relying on a number.
> Items not yet confirmed against a primary source are flagged **⚠ VERIFY**.

---

## Caveats before reading the matrix

- **Baseline only.** Each row shows the *baseline* figure — the simplest case
  for the most common feature type. Regulations usually scale the setback up
  for community wells, classified watercourses, vulnerable aquifers, or
  higher-than-conventional treatment classes.
- **Different reference points.** Some jurisdictions specify the setback from
  the dispersal area edge; others from the **tank**, **building sewer**, or
  **system component generally**. Where it matters, the per-jurisdiction notes
  call this out — and the **tank** and the **dispersal field** often have
  different (smaller / larger) minimums within the same regulation.
- **Component-specific.** A septic tank, holding tank, treatment field, and
  pump chamber can each carry different setbacks.
- **Variances.** Most regimes permit reductions if a Professional / qualified
  designer (or a hydrogeologist, e.g., BC) certifies that public health would
  not be at risk.
- **Vertical separation** (depth to water table / restrictive layer) is a
  separate, equally important rule **not** covered in this comparison.

## What jumps out

- **The 30 m well baseline.** Most of the country clusters around **30 m** for
  the "other" / dug well case (BC, SK, MB, ON, QC, NB, NL). It's the most
  common figure you'll see, and it traces to the EPA's long-standing 100-foot
  guidance.
- **Drilled wells get less.** Jurisdictions that distinguish well construction
  reward properly cased drilled wells with a *shorter* setback — ON 15 m, NS
  15.2 m, NL 16 m, NB 23 m, QC 15 m (sealed). The construction quality
  protects the aquifer, so the dispersal field can be closer.
- **Alberta is the outlier on surface water.** A **90 m** setback from the
  shore of a lake/river/stream/creek for the dispersal component is dramatically
  larger than the 15 m–30.5 m most other provinces use. AB compensates by
  letting *tanks* sit just 10 m away.
- **Atlantic Canada is strict near drinking-water bodies.** NB jumps to **75 m**
  if the surface water is a designated potable supply — far above the typical
  15 m. That's a sensitivity tool the populated, well-water-dependent Atlantic
  provinces lean on.
- **BC is structurally different.** BC doesn't fix a surface-water number in
  the regulation — the SPM bases the dispersal-field-to-surface-water distance
  on percolation rate and a hydrogeological judgement. The 30 m well number is
  a hard regulatory floor; everything else is engineered.
- **The territories are sparse on numbers.** NT and NU rely on the General
  Sanitation Regulations (Public Health Act) plus Environmental Health Officer
  judgement, with MACA northern-engineering guidance for community systems. On
  in-ground dispersal in permafrost, you're often outside the design envelope
  anyway — most communities use holding tanks and trucked service (see
  `provincial_overview.md`).

## Per-jurisdiction notes

### British Columbia
- **Well setback ≥ 30 m (100 ft)** from any well that supplies a domestic
  water system, per the *Sewerage System Regulation*. May be reduced if a
  Professional competent in **hydrogeology** advises the reduced setback will
  not create a health hazard.
- **Tank vs. holding tank:** the SSR uses a 30 m setback for a septic tank
  from the same well; holding-tank setbacks are smaller (commonly 15 m).
- **Surface water:** the regulation does not fix a single distance — the SPM
  V3 sets it via percolation rate and site-specific evaluation by the
  Authorized Person.

### Alberta
- **Tanks** (septic, holding, effluent, lift, settling): ≥ **10 m** from a
  water source/water well, and ≥ **10 m** from a watercourse, per the SOP.
- **Effluent disposal component:** ≥ **90 m (300 ft)** from the shore of a
  lake, river, stream, or creek. _This is the largest fixed surface-water
  setback in any Canadian on-site regime._
- Disposal-field-to-well setbacks scale by well type and treatment class in the
  SOP's tables. ⚠ VERIFY against the SOP 2021 separation-distance tables for
  the case at hand.

### Saskatchewan
- The Department of Health applies a **30 m** separation distance from both
  **surface water** and **water wells** for private sewage works. Variations
  by system type, well construction, and watercourse classification are
  detailed in the *Saskatchewan Onsite Wastewater Disposal Guide* (current
  edition).

### Manitoba
- Under **M.R. 83/2003** (Schedule A), the baseline setback from a shoreline
  or surface watercourse is **30 m (98.4 ft)**, and a greywater pit (and by
  extension other system components) must be ≥ **30 m (100 ft)** from a spring
  or water well, with exceptions for certain well types specified in the
  Schedule.
- Full setback details are in **Schedule A, ss. 1(1)(e) and 2(2)(c)** —
  consult the Schedule for the specific case.

### Ontario
- **OBC Part 8, Table 8.2.1.6.B** (Class 4 — leaching bed distribution
  piping), measured from the centreline of a distribution pipe or leaching
  chamber:
  - Drilled well with watertight casing to ≥ 6 m: **15 m**
  - Other well (dug, bored, etc.): **30 m**
  - Lake, stream, surface water body: **15 m** (each, in separate table rows)
  - Property line: **3 m**

### Québec
- Under **Q-2, r. 22**, an **absorption field** (soil dispersal) requires:
  - Sealed groundwater withdrawal facility: **15 m**
  - Other groundwater withdrawal facility / surface water withdrawal: **30 m**
  - Lakes, watercourses, swamps, ponds: **15 m**
- A well that has been **sealed** in accordance with the Water Withdrawal and
  Protection Regulation (Q-2, r. 35) lets the field be as close as 15 m.
- MRCs, cities, and municipalities can require greater minimums than the
  provincial regulation.

### New Brunswick
- **NB Tech Guidelines (v6), Table 1**, measured from a disposal field (nearest
  distribution line):
  - Drilled well: **23 m (75 ft)**
  - Dug or sandpoint well: **30 m (100 ft)**
  - Water body — not a designated potable supply: **15 m (50 ft)**
  - Water body — designated potable supply: **75 m (250 ft)**
  - Building foundation / foundation drain / any property line: **3.0 m (10 ft)**
- For the **septic tank or pump chamber**: drilled well **15 m**, dug or
  sandpoint well **30 m**.

### Nova Scotia
- Per the **On-site Sewage Disposal Standard** (in force June 2022) and N.S.
  Reg. 194/2007:
  - Drilled well (casing extends ≥ **6.1 m** below surface): **15.2 m** from
    any part of the system (excluding the septic tank, pump or siphon chamber,
    and effluent pipe).
  - Surface watercourse / wetland / marine water body: **30.5 m** from any
    part of the system.

### Prince Edward Island
- Under the **Water Act** Sewage Disposal Systems Regulations (**EC504/21**,
  in force June 16, 2021), the regulation references a Schedule of "Minimum
  Regulatory Requirements" with detail in **Appendix C, Table C1**.
- Confirmed in the public regulation text: the building sewer must be ≥ **3.0 m**
  from a domestic water well; dispersal fields appear to require ≥ **15.2 m
  (50 ft)** from the natural boundary of a body of water.
- ⚠ VERIFY the disposal-field-to-well figures from Appendix C, Table C1
  directly — these did not extract cleanly from the source PDF and are the
  authoritative values.

### Newfoundland & Labrador
- Per the **Sanitation Regulations (CNLR 803/96)** Schedule, the **disposal
  area** setbacks are:
  - Drilled well: **16 m**
  - Dug well: **30 m**
  - Surface water: **30 m**
- These apply to subsurface absorption/disposal fields. Practical design
  guidance is in the *Private Sewage Disposal and Water Supply Standards*
  document.

### Yukon
- Per **O.I.C. 1999/082**:
  - Septic / holding **tank ≥ 15 m** from any potable water source.
  - **No sewage disposal system** within **60 m** of a **community well**.
- The regulation also prohibits surface discharge of untreated sewage or
  effluent.
- ⚠ VERIFY surface-water distance against the *Design Specifications for
  Sewage Disposal Systems*.

### Northwest Territories
- Governed by the **General Sanitation Regulations** under the Public Health
  Act (SNWT 2007, c 17), enforced by **Environmental Health Officers**.
  Engineering guidance for community systems is in MACA's *Good Engineering
  Practice for Northern Water and Sewer Systems*.
- ⚠ VERIFY specific setback distances directly against the General Sanitation
  Regulations — the values were not extractable from public search results and
  the regulation text is best read from the official source.

### Nunavut
- Governed by the **General Sanitation Regulations** (consolidated under the
  Nunavut Public Health Act, SNu 2016, c 13), enforced by **Environmental
  Health Officers**. The regulations are inherited from the pre-division NWT
  framework (R.R.N.W.T. 1990, c P-16), amended for Nunavut.
- In practice, in-ground dispersal is uncommon — most communities use **holding
  tanks** with trucked service, so component-specific setback distances are
  less the active question than tank siting and pump-out logistics.
- ⚠ VERIFY specific values against the consolidated regulation text.
